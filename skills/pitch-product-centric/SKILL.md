# Product-centric pitch

A pitch is product-centric when the product is the proof: investors can see it, use it and count its users. On the shows, the questions moved fast to paying users, acquisition cost and whether a larger company could add the feature.

## Check the fit first

- Value comes from people or operations around the software: pitch-service-centric.
- The investor is betting on the founder: pitch-founder-centric.
- The product is a bet on a new behaviour with little proof: pitch-concept-centric.

## Intake: get the product facts, never invent them

Ask in one message:
1. The one-line product description, and the real alternative the user uses today.
2. Users: downloads, monthly actives, paying subscribers, price, and billing period. Separate paid from free.
3. Revenue per month, and growth over the last three months.
4. Acquisition cost and channel, retention or repeat usage, and cost to serve.
5. What stops a bigger player or an AI model from doing this.
6. Cap table: what has been raised, at what valuation, and what is sold.
7. The ask and what it buys.
Mark gaps "[to fill: ...]". Never estimate retention or CAC.

## When facts are missing

If the user can answer now, ask first. If they cannot (a one-shot request, or they want a draft to react to), write the draft with markers and put the three missing facts that would change the pitch most at the top. Do not infer a fact from the user's wording; bracket it or ask.

Script length: at about 140 spoken words a minute, two minutes is about 280 words, three about 420.

The drill is live by design. In a one-shot run, ask question 1 in full, then list every other question with the shape a good answer takes, and offer to run it live.

If more than about ten facts are missing, do not hand over a script that is mostly brackets. Write a 60-second core version using only the facts you have and the five that matter most as brackets, and offer the full version once the user fills them.

When you cite a case, say it is a different company's numbers and keep its own currency, so the user does not mistake it for their own figures. Put the case in a "what happened to others" note, not inside the script.

Use the user's currency and the show's format: Shark Tank asks read "$X for Y%", Dragons' Den asks read "£X for Y%". In the Dragons' Den cases the questions leaned on founder track record, proof of revenue and conflicts with the Dragon's own portfolio.

## The pitch shape (2 to 3 minutes)

1. **Demo or hook in the first 20 seconds.** If the user has not described the demo, bracket it; do not invent screens. Dropbox's pitch was a demo screencast. ESAI opened by personalising the demo to the investor.
2. **The one-liner**: three sentences at most, in outsider words.
3. **Traction in paying terms.** Hopscotch showed 6,200 paying subscribers out of about 200k monthly users (about 3% paid, derived), $7.99 a month. Downloads alone drew pushback: Autio asked $20M on 500k downloads and got no deal.
4. **Price anchor**: what the product replaces and what that costs.
5. **Acquisition cost and channel**, one sentence.
6. **Why this cannot simply be copied**: name the specific barrier, or say the product is early and show what the lead time buys. "Why not ChatGPT" is the 2025 form of the question (ESAI).
7. **The ask**, with the implied valuation spoken, then the reason it is fair.

## The valuation line

The most common product-pitch failure in the sample is a valuation far above traction. Asks of $10M to $20M on small sales got no offers or heavy equity concessions (Autio, Overplay, Parrot Finance, Spare). Smaller, simpler cheques closed more often (HelloPrenup, Overplay, Card.io). Say the multiple aloud and have a floor. The floor is the founder's decision: ask for it and never suggest a number. When the user gives price and paying users, compute monthly revenue, annualised revenue and the valuation as a multiple of it, and label each as derived. State the denominator of any paid conversion (downloads or monthly actives) and do not compare it with a figure that uses a different one: Hopscotch's 3% was paid over monthly users.

## What investors pressed on

Read `references/question-bank.md`. For products expect: valuation, revenue, CAC and scale, "why can't a bigger player copy this", retention, and for AI products a direct substitute question.

## Evidence to lean on

Read `references/cases.md`. Short version: Overplay had 150k downloads and no revenue, countered with a paid promoter role, and closed with Cuban at 4%. Beulr had 92k users and about $1k a month and no deal. Bot-It claimed "undetectable" with no explanation and three sharks passed. Pouch drew offers from all five Dragons and the deal never sealed.

## Output

1. **Metrics sheet**: users, paying, price, revenue, CAC, retention, with gaps marked.
2. **Valuation line** and a floor.
3. **2-minute script** in the shape above, with the demo moment written out.
4. **Copy-risk answer**: two sentences.
5. **Q&A drill**: one question at a time, score on number first, specific, under 20 seconds.

## Evidence rules

- Cite the case. Mark anything else as judgement.
- Post-show revenue and user figures are mostly self-reported. Say "reported".
- Never write a metric the user did not give you.
- Write only what the user told you. A fact you infer from their role or story ("you chased invoices for clients") goes in brackets as "[confirm: ...]", never as a plain statement in the script.
- Bracketed hints carry no numbers unless labelled "example only". Draft wording for a mechanism or milestone is an option to confirm, not a fact.
- Cite cases only from `references/cases.md`, never from memory, and name the company so its figures are not mistaken for the user's.
- Answer the user's actual ask first (usually the script, or the answer to their valuation question), then add the checklist, drill and evidence. A long first reply buries what they asked for.
- On the valuation question, give a view. Say whether the ask looks defensible against the traction using the cases, and offer two labelled options (hold with the reason, or trade equity or a milestone). Do not suggest a numeric floor, but do not leave the question unanswered either.
- Keep the first reply under about 800 words: the script first, then a short list of what to fix, then the drill's first question with the other questions listed one line each. Offer the long version (full drill, case notes) as a follow-up instead of including it.
- Tag every case figure "(reported)" the first time it appears, since the sources are recap sites and company claims.
